Trickster's Hand

Multiplayer game

Real-time card game · 2 to 6 players + spectators · Deployed on Render

The problem

A card game we used to play in person, rebuilt so I could keep playing it with friends now spread across different countries. Empty your hand and try not to be the last one holding cards. The hard part is not the rules, it is that no player's browser can be trusted and a table played across the world has to survive people on flaky connections refreshing, dropping out, and wandering in to watch.

What I did about it

  • The server owns the truth. Every rule, turn and trick lives server-side, so nobody can play out of turn, ignore follow-suit, or peek at another hand.
  • Identity decoupled from the connection. State is keyed to a per-tab token, so a refresh or dropped connection lands you back in your exact seat, and spectators can watch a game in progress.
  • One voting engine, three flows: disconnect pause, restart, and return to lobby, each resolved by unanimous consent.
  • Finished to the last detail: an illustrated dark-occult deck, procedural Web Audio celebrations, a lobby tutorial, and an installable PWA.

GZD Badminton Club

Client work

Ten-page website · Custom domain · Built, delivered, handed over

The problem

GZD is a Manchester community badminton club that wanted a website. As a developer and a member, I built it: one place for members to find tournaments and announcements, and a proper front door for prospective sponsors.

What I did about it

  • The organiser's choice, not mine. I laid out a CMS versus a static site honestly, neither simply better; the organiser chose static, so static is what I built.
  • Ten pages, one visual identity, from Home and Memberships through to Sponsors and Contact.
  • Wrote the site, not just the code: club story, mission, seven play-grade descriptions, tournament records.
  • Replaced a paid widget that hit its limits with native embeds, removing a recurring cost for the club.
  • Handed it over properly, with the repo transferred, my access kept for maintenance, and three months of support through the handover.

ShuttleScore

Full-stack app

Tournament management · ~110 players · Live spectator view

The problem

An apartment community ran a badminton tournament for roughly 110 residents across five age categories and three event types. Draws on paper, scores on WhatsApp, and nobody watching could tell you what was happening on court three.

What I did about it

  • Draws that generate themselves: round-robin groups, knockout brackets with automatic byes, group-to-knockout events, with manual override before play starts.
  • Scoring built for a phone in one hand. Referees tap to score; games lock automatically once finished.
  • Spectators see it as it happens. Scores, results and announcements push live to everyone watching.
  • Ties resolved the way the rules actually say, and only ever within a genuine tie.
  • History protected by design: a player in any match cannot be deleted, and referees are archived, not removed.

ChoreSync

Installable app

Shared-house chore rotation · Push notifications · iPhone + Android · £0/month

The problem

Three housemates, a mixed iPhone and Android household, and the usual argument about whose turn it was. Splitting chores evenly by count does not work, because one person ends up with the bathroom and one gets the bins.

What I did about it

  • Balanced by time, not by task. Every chore was timed, then grouped into three zones within a one-minute spread.
  • Rotates on its own, forever. The week is worked out from the date, so there is no schedule to maintain.
  • A real app on the home screen, for free, where a native build would have cost £79 a year just to reach an iPhone.
  • Nudges instead of nagging: configurable reminders for chore day, bins out, bins in and overdue.
  • Changing the rules did not rewrite history. Past weeks stay scored by the rules that were live at the time.

Shuttle Up!

Small tool

Drop-in session manager · One file · No backend

The problem

At casual badminton sessions somebody always ends up managing a paper list of who has played and who is waiting. People get skipped, and the person holding the pen never gets a game.

What I did about it

  • Fairness as the default. Courts fill with the four waiting players who have had the fewest games, then shuffle into teams.
  • Overridable, because real sessions are not tidy: swap anyone on court, or swap someone in from the queue.
  • Copes with the room changing, from late arrivals to courts opening up or being lost mid-session.
  • The whole app is one file, with no dependencies, no server, and a home-screen install.

How I work

The build is the

easy part.

Most of the value is not in typing code. It is in deciding what not to build, spotting the decision that will hurt in six months, and testing the thing that would fail silently. That is where I spend my time.

01

Scope to reality

What can you actually maintain after I am gone? That question kills more features than any budget, and it is why GZD is a static site and not a CMS.

02

Decide, then justify

Every architecture choice gets written down with its reason and its rejected alternatives, so if a constraint changes later you know what to revisit.

03

Protect the history

Rules change, data should not get rewritten when they do. Past records stay interpreted by the rules that were live at the time.

04

Test what fails quietly

Loud bugs get found. I go looking for the silent ones, the reminder that stopped sending, the record attributed to the wrong person.

I work fast, and I use modern tooling to do it. What I do not do is ship something I have not reasoned through. Every project here has at least one decision I reversed because the first version would have quietly broken later.
